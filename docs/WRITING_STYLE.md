# Writing Style

This file records observations about YK's writing. It is reference material,
not a checklist. A draft should still follow the subject, language, and format
of the piece being written.

## Reference samples

- `src/content/posts/deploy-minecraft-server-with-docker.md`
  - A practical technical note written from a problem the author actually had.
- `src/content/posts/apple-music-style-navigation-titles.md`
  - A more structured technical explanation built around an observed UI detail.
- `/Volumes/Data/UTokyo/经验分享.md`
  - A long Chinese experience post with timelines, recommendations, uncertainty,
    and strong personal judgments.
- The author's short Chinese social posts
  - Useful for natural rhythm, parenthetical remarks, concrete numbers, and
    unpolished opinions.

These samples represent different formats. Do not make a technical note sound
like an admissions retrospective, or pad a short post until it resembles a
formal essay.

## Shared tendencies

- Begin with the thing that happened, the problem encountered, or the result
  observed. The topic usually does not need an industry-level introduction.
- Prefer concrete details: dates, line counts, limits, commands, percentages,
  failure modes, and the actual choice made.
- State a practical judgment when there is one, then explain its conditions.
  Neutrality is not required.
- Keep uncertainty and imperfect decisions when they were part of the
  experience. Do not rewrite the past as a clean success story.
- Use headings to help readers find information. Sections do not need matching
  lengths, parallel structures, or summaries.
- Parenthetical remarks, brief detours, mixed technical vocabulary, and
  conversational phrasing are natural when they carry the author's actual
  reaction.
- Stop when the useful information has been delivered. An unfinished status or
  a next experiment can be a sufficient ending.

## Common ways a draft drifts

Watch for:

- an opening about an era, industry trend, or universally important problem
  before reaching the author's experience;
- a neat contrast such as "not X, but Y" used to manufacture a thesis;
- several balanced sentences explaining what AI can and cannot do;
- repeated conclusions at the end of sections and again at the end;
- an abstract final paragraph that turns a project note into a manifesto;
- implementation-status sentences that add no useful information to the story;
- three-item lists or symmetrical sections created only because they read
  cleanly;
- replacing specific facts with polished but vague language.

When a paragraph has no new fact, judgment, example, or useful transition,
deleting it is often better than rewriting it.

## Do not imitate mechanically

- Do not insert slang, parentheses, rhetorical questions, or habitual words
  merely to sound like the author.
- Do not preserve grammar mistakes from early English writing.
- Do not ban a sentence pattern globally because it was overused in one draft.
- Do not force every post into first person when a reference note is clearer
  without it.
- Do not trade technical accuracy for surface resemblance.

The goal is to preserve how the author selects facts, makes judgments, and
describes uncertainty—not to reproduce a fixed set of verbal tics.

## Working method

For a substantial rewrite:

1. Extract the facts, examples, and claims from the current post.
2. Separate observed facts from later interpretation.
3. Choose the format that fits the material: technical note, experience post,
   or short update.
4. Draft from the facts rather than paraphrasing the old rhetoric.
5. Remove repeated explanation and any ending that exists only to sound
   complete.
6. Check that translations preserve the same experience and claims without
   requiring identical sentence structure.
